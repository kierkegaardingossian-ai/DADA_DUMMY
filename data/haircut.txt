tips

One might speak of the name Handel in a conversation, to be rhapsoded by one's interlocutor with how deeply and direly he or she loves the symphony The Messiah.

When one truly loves something, one learns about it.  And more than just a little bit.

The author of this thing is of the opinion that in addresses sin issues of churches, one does best doing so much more, and more deeply, with one's own church or denomination, than one points out such in other churches.  And the author of this thing is Anglican.

Anglicans are sadly frequently given over to fetishisms.  In particular, tradition, religious paraphernalia, the arts, scholarship, and the intellect in general.

The fetishisms of these things do so much to detract or even pervert the true love of them.

There is a whole scholarly cottage industry of fetishisms around the Celtic people, while few realize that one of its most famous and scholarly documented moments, the Synod of Whitby, had largely to do with haircuts (tonsures) and the day upon which one should celebrate Easter.  And of course, much of it is - oh so very Anglican.

Fetishisms badly pervert our sensibilities.  And in human experience, there are sensibilities before there are things (Kant, James Joyce).

Adorno on fetishisms in music is a wonderful place to begin.  Many have truly believed they can hear the difference between a Stradivarius and a fine contemporary instrument when in actuality, relatively few not performing the instruments themselves will be able to hear the difference.  The fetishisms around the name Stradivarius cause us to not hear what we are actually hearing. 

Rolland Barthes's essay on The Grain of the Voice brings a wonderful counterpoint to this work of Adorno.  Though Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau's voice was so celebrated - it was a rather reedy, almost ugly, feeble instrument.  Now what he did with that instrument is entirely a different matter.  And this gives us ample pause over the phenomenon of Strad fetishisms - and our own many many many fetishisms causing us to see things which aren't there, feel things which are mostly false, obscure true love, and neglect so many utterly wonderful things which don't carry with them "strad-like" branding.  With the odd additional thought that Fischer-Dieskau became for many a bit of a Stradivarius as well.

Fetishized readings, experiences, exclamations - often flatter ourselves, directly or indirectly.  That I so deeply deeply love Handel's wonderful symphony The Messiah shows indirectly how very deep and devout I am.  Until my interlocutor notices that Handel wrote no symphonies at all - wrong era.  And realizes what a tremendously fetishizing fake I am.

For tradition fetishists - most of us in Anglicanland (the author being no exception) - note how some of us are into designing heraldry for our own names and families - a wonderful and powerful antidote is T.S. Eliot's work Tradition and the Individual Talent - amplified by the amazing intellect and talent of this man, while then comparing what we think of our own minds and "creativity" to what he thought of his own.  And asking us ourselves how we ever got there.  Since when has such smug arrogance been a path to the production or appreciation of beautiful things?  When one has contemplated Eliot's essay long enough to have stripped the arrogance from the notion of "the creative self" into a mere node or catalyst of lived history, one is less inclined to hear things which aren't there which also puff one up.

Gadamer on tradition can be a wonderful supplement - Bilduing, the unheimlich motion out of the little warm home of one's soul, and the return, in some way "domesticating" what one found out there "in the world."

Add to this Eric Hobsbawm on "The Invention of Tradition."

In an interview, E.H. Gombrich noted that his famed text The Story of Art was likely as successful as it was since it demystified art.  Modest demystificatiion is often badly needed for us poor, sinful, fetishizing souls.

To add to the issue of the synod of Whitby:
Many years ago (it seems to have vanished from the internet) - Mark Clavier wrote an article on Katherine Jefferts-Schori's Disneyfying imaginations regarding the Synod of Whitby.  The Rev. Clavier also wrote a history of the Anglican Province of America hosted on the APA's own site - a very very bold move for that small Continuing Anglican Church in its open admission of many of the fallings-short of the church during its history, as well as his relation to the disgraced Bishop Anthony Clavier, who moved back to the Episcopal Church and became a modest yet vocal but also loving proponent of orthodox Christology within that body: https://anglicanrose.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/02/apa-history.pdf .  An honest church tries to be at least somewhat open about its limitations and its corporate fallings-short.
