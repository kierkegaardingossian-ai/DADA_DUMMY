It could be argued that there is a kind of dadaistic formation omperative, obligation, or necessity if western culture is to survive.  Could be - whether actually arguing such is wise is another matter.  And doing so is probably best done in a spirit of "teach the conflicts."

Let us begin with high nineteenth century aestheticism; for example, Walter Pater.  Artistic biography and experience has been functionally deified.  There are the figures of Lord Byron, Matthew Arnold.  M.H. Abrams's book Natural Supernaturalism is relevant here, as Christian scholar Roger Lundin for having taught this at Wheaton College.

Dadaism is often presented as a kind of protest of the Great War and institutions.  But it is also a profound anti-art movement.  One of the few videos available of the Cabaret Voltaire makes this very clear.  The performance begins with a de-tuned piano.  Two women walk on stage - https://youtu.be/fkl92oV1kMc?si=9CwYguIvZn1CUhj4&t=74 .  They are surrounded by dancing monkey men clacking on various clackey type instruments.  After a short framing type dance, they hold their hands in the air, and the first hand gestures appear as if they are framing their own faces.  But then some robotic goofiness occurs with the hands, later their arms, and then their whole bodies, in which they appear to be mirroring each other.  The piano moves from rhythmic music to something a bit creepier, and as the ladies step away from each other, Hugo Ball is revealed behind them in a rather ridiculous suit, with an astounded expression a bit like a little boy ( https://youtu.be/fkl92oV1kMc?si=aa8c6z5OxptokCBz&t=147 ).  He begins reciting the poem Karawane:

jolifanto bambla o falli bambla
großiga m'pfa habla horem
egiga goramen
higo bloiko russula huju
hollaka hollala
anlogo bung

The clacking monkey-men surround him and he is pelted with thrown vegetables.  The clacking monkey men move him off the stage.

Three men appear reciting poems simultanously in a bit of a bouncy way.  Their faces are covered with ugly masks, and the clacking monkey men surround them.

The English translation of the French at the very end reads: "Look at me carefully.  I am a joker.  I am a hoaxer ... Look at me ... I am ugly.  My face is without expression ... I am small ... I am like all of you.

Hugo Ball was very different from Tzara.  Tzara was received in Paris as a kind of glammy splendid hero.  Ball seems to have resisted this being spectacled in this way himself, left the dadaist movement, and became a devout mystical Catholic living in poverty.


Note how explaining this as we have done might be egregiously wrong - Borges's map.
