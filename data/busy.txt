THE GREAT BUSYNESS MACHINE
DUMMY Dadaist Polyphonic Helical Musical Formation Exercise

text [ note, the song is spread over two separate videos, Part 1 and Part 2 ]:

BUSY! BUSY! BUSY!
The Great Busyness Machine

An unSong in eleven movements, several interruptions, and one suspicious silence.

hints:

Governing intention
Investigate busyness as an obstacle to intellectual and personal formation.
Distinguish actual demands on time from performed busyness.
Explore the social prestige of being busy.
Investigate busyness as identity, habit, defense, excuse, and avoidance.
Encourage participants to examine their relationship with time rather than simply manage it better.
Do not assume that all busyness is voluntary.
Do not shame people experiencing poverty, caregiving responsibilities, demanding employment, illness, or other genuine constraints.
Avoid reducing formation to productivity improvement.
Ask whether formation requires something that conventional productivity culture cannot easily measure.
Make room for humor, embarrassment, recognition, disagreement, and genuine discovery.

Draw upon, but do not mechanically summarize:

Leisure versus mere recovery for further work.
The distinction between contemplation and productivity.
Leisure: The Basis of Culture.

Hannah Arendt

Labor, work, and action.
The human condition.

Seneca: On the Shortness of Life; the difference between having a long life and using one's life.
Augustine: memory, time, and the difficulty of understanding the present.
William James: attention and the selection of experience.

3. Important distinctions

Explore the following without assuming that one side is always correct:

Busy versus productive.
Productive versus meaningful.
Activity versus formation.
Occupation versus attention.
Urgency versus importance.
Necessary work versus self-imposed work.
Real scarcity of time versus theatrical scarcity.
Leisure versus laziness.
Rest versus escape.
Contemplation versus passive consumption.
Scheduling time versus inhabiting time.
Having no time versus not making time.
Being needed versus needing to feel needed.
Accomplishment versus becoming.
Talking about work versus doing work.
Doing work versus talking about doing work.
4. The social performance of busyness

Investigate:

The conversational opening: "I've been so busy!"
The competitive exchange of schedules.
The person who cannot accept an invitation without announcing an impossibly full calendar.
The humblebrag of exhaustion.
The prestige of being indispensable.
The status of the permanently overcommitted.
The subtle insult implied by "I simply haven't had time."
The person who is always rushing but never seems to arrive.
The difference between being genuinely overwhelmed and cultivating an identity around overwhelm.
Busyness as a way of avoiding the question: "What are you actually doing with your life?"
5. The Dadaist laboratory

Encourage unexpected transformations:

The calendar as a religious text.
The smartphone as a portable taskmaster.
The meeting as a ceremonial dance.
The inbox as an invasive species.
The deadline as a small domestic deity.
The exhausted person as a national monument.
The to-do list as an unfinished autobiography.
The clock as a tyrannical percussion instrument.
The coffee cup as a sacramental vessel of productivity.
The empty afternoon as a suspected criminal.
The person who has no time to think as a professional thinker.
The calendar that eats its owner.
A meeting about finding time to have meetings.
A productivity expert who cannot explain what productivity is for.

Use absurdity to reveal assumptions, not merely to entertain.

6. Polyphonic voices

Create distinct voices that disagree, overlap, interrupt, and occasionally misunderstand one another.

Possible participants:

THE BUSY PERSON: genuinely overwhelmed.
THE BUSY PERSON'S PUBLICIST: announces every obligation.
THE CALENDAR: believes every empty space is a vacancy requiring occupation.
THE CLOCK: impatient, mechanical, relentless.
THE INBOX: multiplies without permission.
THE BOSS: measures visible activity.
THE FRIEND: asks when they last had an uninterrupted conversation.
THE CHILD: asks why adults are always going somewhere.
THE PHILOSOPHER: asks what all this activity is for.
THE MONK: recommends silence but has a complicated relationship with the institution of monastic life.
THE ARTIST: makes something unnecessary.
THE HISTORIAN: remembers that other societies have organized time differently.
THE AI: produces schedules, efficiency strategies, and increasingly elaborate methods of finding time to use the schedules.
THE UNEMPLOYED OR UNDEREMPLOYED PERSON: challenges the assumption that being busy is universally available or desirable.
THE FUTURE SELF: asks what the present self has been postponing.
SILENCE: does not explain itself.

Do not make all voices equally wise. Let them expose one another's limitations.

8. The helical structure

Return repeatedly to the same central phrases, changing their meaning.

Suggested recurring phrases:

"I haven't got time."
"I'm just so busy."
"Something important has come up."
"I'll get to that later."
"I must make better use of my time."
"I need some time to think."
"When things settle down..."
"There are only twenty-four hours in a day."

Each return should introduce a new perspective.

The phrase "I haven't got time" might initially express genuine distress, subsequently become a status announcement, then an excuse, then an admission, and finally a question.

Do not explain every transformation.

Let the music do some of the work.

9. Formation questions
What would happen if you stopped announcing your busyness?
What activities would disappear if nobody could admire you for doing them?
Which commitments are genuinely necessary?
Which commitments have become part of your identity?
What important encounter is repeatedly postponed?
What do you do when nobody is demanding anything from you?
Are you afraid of an empty afternoon?
Can you distinguish being occupied from being alive?
What does your calendar know about you that you would rather not admit?
What would you do if you could not use busyness as an explanation?
What does formation require that cannot be squeezed into the leftover minutes?
10. The practical formation challenge

Do not prescribe a universal schedule.

Instead, invite participants to undertake a modest experiment:

Identify one recurring claim of busyness.
Examine the actual activity behind it.
Identify one activity that might be reduced, refused, delegated, or reconsidered, where circumstances permit.
Protect one small interval for undirected attention or serious inquiry.
Do not immediately fill that interval with another productivity exercise.
Return to the original question after several days.
Ask whether anything has changed in the person's relationship with time.
11. Important safeguards
Do not romanticize idleness.
Do not imply that people with demanding lives lack seriousness.
Do not suggest that all time constraints are self-created.
Do not confuse privilege with virtue.
Do not make leisure another competitive achievement.
Do not turn contemplation into another productivity technique.
Do not conclude that everyone should simply work less.
Preserve the tension between necessary activity and the need for formation.
12. Desired outcome

Not a better calendar.

Not a more efficient person.

Not a new method for squeezing additional tasks into the day.

Rather:

A person who has become more attentive to the difference between what must be done, what is habitually done, what is socially performed, and what is being indefinitely postponed.

An encounter with the possibility that time is not merely something to manage.

It is something to live.

End without a tidy resolution.
